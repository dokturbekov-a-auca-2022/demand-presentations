Opening bid

Which small thing makes a day feel richer?

Choose one. Tell a partner: "It makes my day better because..." Imaginary answers are welcome.
Your opening bid will appear here.
Use this as a low-risk check-in. Students may choose an imagined answer. Invite two volunteers to extend with because.

Bridge from Lesson 12

If you had $1,000, where would the value go?

Choose one first. Then make a second conditional sentence and add a reason.

Bid formula

If I had $1,000, I would ... because...

Elicit one second conditional sentence as a bridge. Today the language focus shifts to reasons, examples and respectful responses.

Silent position

Can money buy happiness?

Choose your starting position. You may move after hearing stronger evidence.
No position is "the correct answer."
Frame this as an opening position, not a test or disclosure. Do not count votes publicly unless the class is comfortable.

Look first. Then choose a lot.
Give silent looking time. Encourage careful language: can, may and often are safer than always or never.

Tap each item to cycle: NEED -> WANT -> BOTH. Defend one difficult choice.
Sorted: 0 / 9.
Accept different positions when the reason is clear. Point out that context can change a want into a need.

Debate language

Build, connect, and challenge ideas.

Open each catalog tab. Select one phrase and complete it aloud.
Choose language that challenges the idea, not the person.
Model warm intonation. Students should never use disagreement language to criticise a classmate personally.

Argument workshop

A bid becomes believable in three moves.

Claim

Money can support some happiness.

+
Reason

It can reduce stress around basic needs.

+
Example

A safe home can help a family feel secure.

Claim + reason + example = an argument people can follow.
Read the model. Replace one part to create a new argument.
Keep the focus on the argument shape. Accept simple vocabulary if the logical connection is clear.

Precision game

Which claim is careful enough to defend?

Choose the strongest claim. Explain why can and when make it more believable.
One claim can survive questions. Find it.
Elicit why always is risky. Contrast strong thinking with loud or absolute language.

Pre-listening

Two good Saturdays. Two different kinds of value.

Select two predictions. Listen to confirm or revise them.
Predictions selected: 0 / 2.
Do not correct predictions yet. Ask students what details they expect to hear about money, pride, friends or family.

Listening story

Where does each good feeling come from?

Sam worked at a cafe for three hours. He saved money and bought second-hand headphones. He felt proud because he had earned them. Lina cooked with her aunt, played football with friends and called her grandmother. She spent almost no money, but she laughed a lot. Both teenagers said their Saturday was good.
First listen: identify each source of happiness. Second listen: note one money benefit and one non-money benefit.
Play twice. Keep the transcript blurred until students compare answers after the second listen.

Listening check

Choose the answer the story supports.

What is the message?

Answer alone, compare with a partner, then tap.
Accurate answers: 0 / 3.
Ask students to cite the detail they heard before they tap. Replay the relevant sentence if needed.

Evidence auction

Useful evidence, weak evidence, or a question?

Open three clues. For each, say: useful, limited, or needs a question.
Evidence inspected: 0 / 5.
Treat each visual as a prompt, not proof. Students should identify what additional information would make a claim stronger.

Team FOR

Money can buy or support some happiness.

Choose two reasons. Add one example and one limit: "However, money cannot..."
Select two evidence cards. Prepare an opener and a balanced final sentence.
Clarify that FOR does not mean "money is everything." Reward fair limits and concrete examples.

Team AGAINST

Money cannot buy every important kind of happiness.

Choose two reasons. Add one example and one concession: "Of course, money is important for..."
Select two evidence cards. Prepare a respectful response to the FOR team.
Clarify that AGAINST should acknowledge the importance of basic needs. Encourage nuanced language.

Balance the scale

Can both teams be partly right?

Money can...

meet needs, reduce some stress and create choices.

Money cannot guarantee...

trust, health, time, purpose or strong relationships.

Work in pairs. Combine both sides into one balanced conclusion.
"Money can support happiness, but it cannot create every kind of happiness."
Have pairs improve the model with one example. The goal is not to erase disagreement but to recognise complexity.

Team roles

Every voice has one clear job.

Choose a role. Rehearse your first sentence with your team.
Roles claimed: 0 / 4.

Perspective switch

Defend one useful point from the other side.

"One point I understand from the other side is ... because ..."

Choose one prompt. Give the strongest fair version of the opposite argument.
Understanding an idea does not mean you must personally agree.
This is an empathy and reasoning task, not a forced opinion change. Praise accurate representation of the other side.

Audience coaching

Reward language moves, not personalities.

Choose two stamps. Give one specific sentence of feedback to a speaker.
Feedback stamps selected: 0 / 2.
Model specific feedback: "Your bus-ticket example made the claim clear." Avoid ranking students or commenting on confidence as personality.

Final vote

Did the evidence move your position?

Vote again. Then complete one reflection sentence.
A more detailed opinion is progress, even if your position stays the same.
Do not require students to announce a personal vote. They can reflect through a fictional speaker or submit privately.
